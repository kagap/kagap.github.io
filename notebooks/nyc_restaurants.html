Inside New York's restaurant inspections, in plain SQL¶

163,000 inspection records covering 26,000 restaurants (January 2025 onwards) from the city's health department, analysed with SQL only. The table has one row per violation, so the first step of most queries is a CTE that collapses it to one row per inspection. Then: grade shares by borough, the most common violations, medians per cuisine built from ROW_NUMBER windows, monthly trends with LAG, chain rankings from both ends with two ROW_NUMBERs, closure rates, and what happens at the next inspection after a bad one (LEAD). Python only runs the queries and draws the charts.

Data: DOHMH New York City Restaurant Inspection Results from NYC Open Data. Run python build_db.py once to download everything since 1 January 2025 (about 33 MB) into a SQLite database, then point RESTAURANTS_DB at that file.

Reading the data. A lower score is better: 0-13 earns an A, 14-27 a B and 28 or more a C. Not every inspection gets a letter (grades are given after the initial inspection or a re-inspection, and a restaurant can be left "pending"), so grade shares use inspections that did receive an A, B or C. Rows with a missing borough are left out of the borough comparison.

1. How do the boroughs compare on grades?¶

First a CTE reduces the table to one row per inspection. Then conditional aggregation counts A, B and C grades per borough and the shares come straight from averaging 0/1 flags (AVG(grade = 'A')).

In [3]:
grades = q("""
WITH insp AS (
    SELECT camis, inspection_date,
           MAX(dba)                          AS name,
           MAX(boro)                         AS boro,
           MAX(cuisine_description)          AS cuisine,
           MAX(inspection_type)              AS inspection_type,
           MAX(score)                        AS score,
           MAX(grade)                        AS grade,
           MAX(action)                       AS action,
           COUNT(violation_code)             AS violations,
           SUM(critical_flag = 'Critical')   AS critical_violations
    FROM inspections
    GROUP BY camis, inspection_date
)
SELECT boro                                              AS borough,
       COUNT(*)                                          AS graded_inspections,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(grade = 'A'), 1)                AS a_pct,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(grade = 'B'), 1)                AS b_pct,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(grade = 'C'), 1)                AS c_pct,
       ROUND(AVG(score), 1)                              AS avg_score
FROM insp
WHERE grade IN ('A', 'B', 'C') AND boro <> '0'
GROUP BY boro
ORDER BY a_pct DESC
""")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8.5, 3.9))
ax.barh(grades.borough[::-1], grades.a_pct[::-1], color="#38a169", label="A")
ax.barh(grades.borough[::-1], grades.b_pct[::-1], left=grades.a_pct[::-1], color=AMBER, label="B")
ax.barh(grades.borough[::-1], grades.c_pct[::-1], left=(grades.a_pct + grades.b_pct)[::-1], color="#c0392b", label="C")
for y, a in enumerate(grades.a_pct[::-1]):

ax.set_xlabel("% of graded inspections")

2. What do inspectors find most often?¶

Each violation code is counted once per inspection (COUNT(DISTINCT ...)) and divided by the total number of inspections from a scalar subquery. The lookup table supplies the description (shortened here).

In [4]:
violations = q("""
SELECT v.violation_code                                          AS code,
       substr(c.violation_description, 1, 80)                    AS description,
       MAX(v.critical_flag)                                      AS type,
       COUNT(DISTINCT v.camis || v.inspection_date)              AS inspections,
       ROUND(100.0 * COUNT(DISTINCT v.camis || v.inspection_date)
             / (SELECT COUNT(DISTINCT camis || inspection_date) FROM inspections), 1) AS pct_of_inspections
FROM inspections v
JOIN violation_codes c USING (violation_code)
GROUP BY v.violation_code
ORDER BY inspections DESC
LIMIT 10
""")

3. Which cuisines score best and worst?¶

SQLite has no MEDIAN, so it is built from a window: ROW_NUMBER() orders each cuisine's inspections by score and COUNT(*) OVER gives the group size, so the row half-way down is the median. Only cuisines with 400 or more scored inspections are included.

In [5]:
cuisines = q("""
WITH insp AS (
    SELECT camis, inspection_date,
           MAX(dba)                          AS name,
           MAX(boro)                         AS boro,
           MAX(cuisine_description)          AS cuisine,
           MAX(inspection_type)              AS inspection_type,
           MAX(score)                        AS score,
           MAX(grade)                        AS grade,
           MAX(action)                       AS action,
           COUNT(violation_code)             AS violations,
           SUM(critical_flag = 'Critical')   AS critical_violations
    FROM inspections
    GROUP BY camis, inspection_date
),
scored AS (
    SELECT cuisine, score, critical_violations
    FROM insp
    WHERE score IS NOT NULL AND cuisine IS NOT NULL
),
ranked AS (
    SELECT *,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (PARTITION BY cuisine ORDER BY score) AS rn,
           COUNT(*)     OVER (PARTITION BY cuisine)                AS n
    FROM scored
)
SELECT cuisine,
       MAX(n)                                                        AS inspections,
       MAX(CASE WHEN rn = (n + 1) / 2 THEN score END)                AS median_score,
       ROUND(AVG(score), 1)                                          AS avg_score,
       ROUND(AVG(critical_violations), 1)                            AS avg_critical_violations
FROM ranked
GROUP BY cuisine
HAVING inspections >= 400
ORDER BY median_score DESC, avg_score DESC
""")

d = pd.concat([cuisines.head(10), cuisines.tail(10)])

Monthly inspection counts and average scores for cycle inspections, with LAG() giving the change in the average score from the previous month.

monthly = q("""
WITH insp AS (
    SELECT camis, inspection_date,
           MAX(dba)                          AS name,
           MAX(boro)                         AS boro,
           MAX(cuisine_description)          AS cuisine,
           MAX(inspection_type)              AS inspection_type,
           MAX(score)                        AS score,
           MAX(grade)                        AS grade,
           MAX(action)                       AS action,
           COUNT(violation_code)             AS violations,
           SUM(critical_flag = 'Critical')   AS critical_violations
    FROM inspections
    GROUP BY camis, inspection_date
),
monthly AS (
    SELECT substr(inspection_date, 1, 7)           AS month,
           COUNT(*)                                AS inspections,
           ROUND(AVG(score), 1)                    AS avg_score,
           ROUND(100.0 * AVG(grade = 'A'), 1)      AS a_grade_pct
    FROM insp
    WHERE inspection_type LIKE 'Cycle Inspection%' AND score IS NOT NULL
    GROUP BY month
)
SELECT month, inspections, avg_score, a_grade_pct,
       ROUND(avg_score - LAG(avg_score) OVER (ORDER BY month), 1) AS change_vs_prev_month
FROM monthly
ORDER BY month
""")

5. Which chains have the cleanest and the dirtiest records?¶

Restaurants are grouped by trading name, and only names with at least 15 locations count as chains. Two ROW_NUMBERs, one ascending and one descending by average score, let a single WHERE pick the best and the worst eight from the same ranked table.

In [7]:
chains = q("""
WITH insp AS (
    SELECT camis, inspection_date,
           MAX(dba)                          AS name,
           MAX(boro)                         AS boro,
           MAX(cuisine_description)          AS cuisine,
           MAX(inspection_type)              AS inspection_type,
           MAX(score)                        AS score,
           MAX(grade)                        AS grade,
           MAX(action)                       AS action,
           COUNT(violation_code)             AS violations,
           SUM(critical_flag = 'Critical')   AS critical_violations
    FROM inspections
    GROUP BY camis, inspection_date
),
chains AS (
    SELECT name,
           COUNT(DISTINCT camis)                   AS locations,
           COUNT(*)                                AS inspections,
           ROUND(AVG(score), 1)                    AS avg_score,
           ROUND(100.0 * AVG(grade = 'A'), 1)      AS a_grade_pct
    FROM insp
    WHERE score IS NOT NULL
    GROUP BY name
    HAVING locations >= 15
),
ranked AS (
    SELECT *,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY avg_score)      AS best_rank,
           ROW_NUMBER() OVER (ORDER BY avg_score DESC) AS worst_rank
    FROM chains
)
SELECT CASE WHEN best_rank <= 8 THEN 'Best' ELSE 'Worst' END AS group_,
       name, locations, inspections, avg_score, a_grade_pct
FROM ranked
WHERE best_rank <= 8 OR worst_rank <= 8
ORDER BY avg_score
""")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(9, 6))
ax.barh(chains.name[::-1], chains.avg_score[::-1], color=[AMBER if g == "Worst" else BLUE for g in chains.group_[::-1]])
for y, (v, n) in enumerate(zip(chains.avg_score[::-1], chains.locations[::-1])):
    ax.text(v + 0.3, y, f"{v:.1f}  ({n} locations)", va="center", fontsize=8)
ax.set_xlim(0, chains.avg_score.max() * 1.3)
ax.set_title("Chains with the best (blue) and worst (amber) average inspection score")
ax.tick_params(axis="y", labelsize=8)
fig.tight_layout()
plt.show()

6. Which cuisines get closed most often?¶

An inspection ends in a closure when its action text starts with 'Establishment Closed'. Averaging that 0/1 flag per cuisine gives the closure rate (cuisines with at least 600 inspections).

closures = q("""
WITH insp AS (
    SELECT camis, inspection_date,
           MAX(dba)                          AS name,
           MAX(boro)                         AS boro,
           MAX(cuisine_description)          AS cuisine,
           MAX(inspection_type)              AS inspection_type,
           MAX(score)                        AS score,
           MAX(grade)                        AS grade,
           MAX(action)                       AS action,
           COUNT(violation_code)             AS violations,
           SUM(critical_flag = 'Critical')   AS critical_violations

LIMIT 10

7. What happens at the next inspection after a bad one?¶

LEAD(score) and LEAD(inspection_date) over each restaurant's inspections (ordered by date) put the next inspection on the same row as a failing one (score 28 or more, a C). A CASE groups them by how bad the first score was.

In [9]:
recovery = q("""
WITH insp AS (
    SELECT camis, inspection_date,
           MAX(dba)                          AS name,
           MAX(boro)                         AS boro,
           MAX(cuisine_description)          AS cuisine,
           MAX(inspection_type)              AS inspection_type,
           MAX(score)                        AS score,
           MAX(grade)                        AS grade,
           MAX(action)                       AS action,
           COUNT(violation_code)             AS violations,
           SUM(critical_flag = 'Critical')   AS critical_violations
    FROM inspections
    GROUP BY camis, inspection_date
),
sequence AS (
    SELECT camis, inspection_date, score,
           LEAD(score)           OVER (PARTITION BY camis ORDER BY inspection_date) AS next_score,
           LEAD(inspection_date) OVER (PARTITION BY camis ORDER BY inspection_date) AS next_date
    FROM insp
    WHERE score IS NOT NULL
)
SELECT CASE WHEN score >= 60 THEN '3  60 or more'
            WHEN score >= 40 THEN '2  40-59'
            ELSE                  '1  28-39' END                       AS first_score,
       COUNT(*)                                                       AS cases,
       ROUND(AVG(julianday(next_date) - julianday(inspection_date))) AS avg_days_to_next,
       ROUND(AVG(next_score), 1)                                      AS avg_next_score,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(next_score <= 13), 1)                        AS next_is_a_pct,
       ROUND(100.0 * AVG(next_score >= 28), 1)                        AS next_still_c_pct
FROM sequence
WHERE score >= 28 AND next_score IS NOT NULL
GROUP BY first_score
ORDER BY first_score
""")

fig, ax = plt.subplots(figsize=(8, 3.9))
x = np.arange(len(recovery))
ax.bar(x - 0.2, recovery.next_is_a_pct, width=0.4, color="#38a169", label="Next inspection is an A")

ax.set_xticks(x, recovery.first_score.str.slice(3))
ax.set_xlabel("Score at the failing inspection")
ax.set_ylabel("% of cases")
ax.set_title("Outcome of the next inspection after a score of 28 or more")
ax.legend(frameon=False)

What the queries say¶

  • Most restaurants get an A, but the boroughs differ. Staten Island (89.7% A) and Manhattan (89.6%) are on top, then Brooklyn (88.8%), the Bronx (87.1%) and Queens (84.1%). Queens also has the most C grades (5.6%) and the highest average score (12.7, against 11.0 in Staten Island).
  • The most common finding is a cleaning issue, not a food hazard. Non-food contact surfaces that are not kept clean (code 10F) are cited in 49.8% of inspections. Pests come next: conditions conducive to rodents or insects (08A) are cited in 28.3% of inspections, evidence of mice in 15.6% and of roaches in 11.9%. The most common critical violations are food not protected from contamination (24.5%) and cold food held above 41 degrees F (24.0%).
  • Cuisine matters for the score. Indian restaurants have the highest median inspection score (20, where lower is better), followed by Latin American, Thai and Asian fusion (18 each), and donut shops have the lowest (10), then hamburger and Tex-Mex places (11). Menus with more raw preparation have more points to check, which may explain part of the gap (an interpretation, the data only shows the difference).
  • Closures follow a similar pattern. 4.8% of Indian restaurants' inspections ended in a closure, 3.5% of Caribbean and 3.0% of Chinese ones (126 closures, the most among these ten cuisines).
  • Scores are higher in summer. The monthly average for cycle inspections ranged from 14.9 (February 2025) to 20.3 (July 2026). In both years it was 15-16 in the winter months and 18-20 from June to September, a repeating seasonal pattern (the latest month in the data is incomplete).
  • Big chains are mostly clean, with exceptions. The best average scores among chains with at least 15 locations belong to Wonder (6.8), Starbucks Coffee (7.3) and 7th Street Burger (8.1). Starbucks appears under two spellings in the data, which is why names can split a chain. At the bottom, EarthBar and Halal Munchies average 29.2 with only half their inspections graded A, Kennedy Fried Chicken (66 locations) averages 18.7 and 76% A.
  • A bad inspection is usually followed by recovery. After a failing score of 28-39, 54.4% of restaurants get an A at the next inspection (after 99 days on average) and 18.9% fail again. The worst performers (60 or more) are re-inspected much sooner (41 days on average), and 57.9% reach an A next time, though 23.6% are still at C level.

Limits: the data starts in January 2025; the source has one row per violation, so inspections were rebuilt with a CTE; some inspections have no grade (pending or not yet issued), so grade shares cover only A, B and C; chain names are as typed by the inspectors, so spelling variants split chains; differences between cuisines and boroughs are descriptive, not a judgement of any single restaurant.

Techniques covered: CTEs that change the grain of a table, conditional aggregation, ROW_NUMBER percentiles, LAG, two opposite ROW_NUMBERs, LEAD and CASE bands.
